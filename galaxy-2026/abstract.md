# Abstract — The Galaxy ACCESS-CI Gateway 2026/27 allocation

For two decades, the Galaxy Project (<https://galaxyproject.org/>) has worked to solve key issues plaguing modern data-intensive biology—the inability of researchers to access cutting-edge analysis methods, to share analysis results transparently, and to precisely reproduce complex computational analyses. Due to the generous support of ACCESS-CI, Galaxy has become one of the largest and most widely used open source platforms for biological data science. Promoting openness and collaboration in all facets of the project, from technical decisions to training and leadership, has enabled us to build a vibrant community of users, developers, system engineers, and educators who continuously contribute new software features, add the latest tools, adopt the most modern infrastructure, author training materials, and lead research and training workshops. Genomics research is continuously evolving, and current challenges include the rapid growth in size and complexity of new datasets and the continuing expansion in the breadth of research areas capable of generating high throughput data.

Most importantly, Galaxy is **not** a project led by a single PI, lab, or institution. It is a community of users, developers, and infrastructure administrators that work together to provide a unique, freely available, high performance data analysis environment serving the full range of biological questions as well as other disciplines (see <https://galaxyproject.org/use/>). This allocation request aims at sustaining and extending the capability of our service to support the full range of data analysis scenarios present in modern biology.

During the current allocation period the gateway has additionally served as the computational backbone for two flagship community efforts: completion of Phase 1 of the Vertebrate Genomes Project, and the launch of GenomeArk2 (<https://genomeark2.org>), an open, petabyte-scale distribution platform for VGP data built on ACCESS-CI object storage. Together these move both large-scale genome assembly and the downstream analysis of its products onto public national cyberinfrastructure rather than commercial clouds.
